04 · What did we learn?
🚗
Traffic alone doesn't set pollution

States with the heaviest traffic are not automatically the most polluted. Traffic is associated with pollution — but it doesn't tell the whole story.

❄️
Winter is the worst season

Pollution peaks cluster in winter, when cold air tends to trap exhaust near the ground — especially in valley states like Utah.

🌟
Busy but clean is possible

Some states combine heavy traffic with comparatively low pollution per vehicle — consistent with cleaner-running fleets and cleaner energy.

⚠️
A few states face a double burden

A small group combines high traffic and high pollution — the clearest candidates for cleaner-fleet policy.

🔬 Methodology & data notes

Data. Monthly state-level averages, Jan 2015 – Dec 2025, joined from U.S. DOT FHWA traffic counts and EPA AirData monitoring (50 states + Washington, D.C.; 6,700 records). Reporting gaps (<2.5%) are flagged, not imputed.

Pollution relative to traffic. A descriptive proxy: PM2.5 divided by traffic volume. It helps separate busy-but-clean states from busy-and-polluted ones — it is not a direct measurement of vehicle maintenance, fleet age, or emissions technology.

Statistics. The dashed line on the scatter plot is an ordinary least-squares fit; the correlation number compares state-average traffic with the selected metric. All values are state-level monthly averages — individual cities and roads can differ sharply, and state-level patterns do not describe any individual community.

Interpretation. Every statement on this site is associative: higher traffic tends to accompany higher pollution on average. Nothing here establishes that traffic causes pollution or that fleet programs cause improvements — weather, geography, industry, and monitoring coverage all play a role.
